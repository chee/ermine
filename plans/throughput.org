#+title: Subduction ephemeral messages: throughput and latency

Measured 2026-10-07 from London (Wi-Fi, 531 Mbit/s down, 47 Mbit/s up,
=networkQuality=), Deno 2.8.2, =npm:@automerge/subduction@0.23.0=.
Script: =ts/deno/bench_ephemeral.ts= (=deno run -A deno/bench_ephemeral.ts
ping|flow|burst|word --via sub|ws|tcp --procs 1|2 …=). Both peers on this
machine, so one-way times use one clock. Payload bytes published to
galaxy.observer over the whole session: about 7.8 MB, each delivered once
more downstream. That's more than the "few MB" asked for.

* Answers
- *The ~30 s stall is in Deno's WebSocket client, not the server.* After a
  burst bigger than the socket's send buffer (macOS =tcp.sendspace= =
  131072), the frames Deno had to queue (=bufferedAmount= peaked at exactly
  their size) stay unsent until this client *writes* again. The next write
  is the pong to the server's first keepalive ping, which comes 30 s after
  connecting, so the frames turn up ~29.6 s later. Evidence:
  - It happens on a fresh connection: 10×16 kB stalled, 8×16 kB did not.
    16×16 kB stalled 3 times out of 3, always the last 4 frames (64 608 B).
  - When the sender sends one more 16-byte message 3 s after the burst
    (=--nudge 3000=), the stuck frames arrive at 3.07–3.18 s, followed by
    that message.
  - When the *receiver* sends to the sender at 3 s (=--poke=), nothing
    changes: the frames still arrive at 29.6 s. So incoming data doesn't
    free them; only an outgoing write does.
  - The same burst from Node 24 (undici WebSocket, same wasm package)
    arrived whole, twice (16×16 kB and 24×16 kB). A local Subduction
    server over plain ws, and a local Deno TLS server that stops reading,
    did not reproduce it.
  - A long-running connection's send buffer grows (autotuning), so a later
    burst of the same size often passes, which is why it looked erratic.
  - Pacing avoids it: on a fresh connection, 16×16 kB sent 20 ms apart
    delivered all of them (max latency 39 ms, 718 kB/s). A steady flow
    never stalled either, because every send flushes the ones before it.
  - Workaround if needed: after a burst, send a small message (or keep a
    beat going) so Deno writes again. The 1024 queues and the per-peer
    dispatch cap (512) are not involved. No loss at that size.
  - Not found as a known Deno issue (one search); worth a minimal repro for
    denoland/deno.
- *Word topics work.* A topic is any 32 bytes (=Topic.fromBytes= only checks
  the length). In two processes, each peer derived its topic from
  =sha256("ermine channel:kitchen")= = =dbebec87…0fc9f82b= (it matches
  =shasum=), and five pings out of five came back. Anyone who knows the
  word can read and write the topic, and galaxy's policy is open.
- *Order*: through galaxy, reordering grows with rate (100 B at 1000/s:
  915 of 2000 arrived out of order). A direct WebSocket and TCP never
  reordered.
- *Ceiling through galaxy*: about 300–340 kB/s sustained per sender at
  1 kB and up. About 1000 msgs/s at 100 B, with loss at 2000/s. Above
  the ceiling, messages queue in the sender's Deno (latency climbs to
  2 s); small ones are also dropped. My uplink is 47 Mbit/s, and the same
  clients through a local (debug) Subduction server reached 30 MB/s, so
  the limit is on galaxy's side: Railway's proxy or the container. I did
  not find which.

* Baselines on this machine (2 s each; highest clean rate, then the ceiling)

The direct paths never lost or reordered anything. Each "break" is the
single-threaded sender running out of CPU: it builds a random payload per
message and the receiver falls behind. The links themselves were not
saturated.

Losses only happen with Subduction, and only when it is overrun. The likely
place is its bounded queues: the ephemeral inbound channel of 1024, and a
WebSocket outbound channel of 1024.

* Not measured: a real peer-to-peer connection
A WebRTC data channel between two browsers over the internet was *not*
measured. Estimates:
- *Latency*: relaying sends A→server→B. Each message pays the client–server
  hop twice: ~18 ms one way here, ~37 ms round trip. A direct path pays
  A→B once. Two peers in one city, or on one LAN, would see roughly 1–10 ms
  instead of ~18 ms one way. For peers far apart, a direct path saves the
  detour to the server (Railway; edge in London, backend region unknown),
  and the server's own queueing and reordering.
- *Throughput*: direct would lose galaxy's ~300 kB/s ceiling and be bounded
  by the slower peer's uplink and SCTP over DTLS. Neither was measured.
- *NAT*: a direct path is not always possible. Estimates for how many
  WebRTC sessions fall back to a TURN relay range from 10–25% (more on
  corporate networks) ([[https://www.forasoft.com/learn/video-streaming/glossary/terms-streaming/turn][forasoft]]); 85–90% need no TURN ([[https://www.nojitter.com/know-where-turn-when-deploying-webrtc][No Jitter]]). Those sessions are back to two
  hops through a relay, just a TURN server instead of Subduction. Setting
  up the connection also needs a signalling channel (which could be a
  Subduction topic) and an ICE exchange, typically several round trips
  before the first byte.
- Ordering: a WebRTC data channel can be ordered and reliable, so the
  link's own reordering and gap handling would be unnecessary there.

* Caveats
- One afternoon's run, over Wi-Fi, against a shared server; galaxy numbers
  varied between runs (the first seconds of a connection were slower).
- =bufferedAmount= in Deno counts frames waiting in Deno's queue; once a
  frame passes to the socket it reads 0, even in the stall.
- The galaxy server's version is unknown; the local server is subduction
  at 2566cbf, debug build.
